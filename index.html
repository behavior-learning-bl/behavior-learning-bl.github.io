The idea in one sentence

Learn the optimization structure behind the data. BL models observed responses through learnable utility-maximization problems, combining predictive performance with intrinsic interpretability and, in its identifiable variant, principled statistical inference.

Abstract

Inspired by behavioral science, we propose Behavior Learning (BL), a novel general-purpose machine learning framework that learns interpretable and identifiable optimization structures from data, ranging from single optimization problems to hierarchical compositions. It unifies predictive performance, intrinsic interpretability, and identifiability, with broad applicability to scientific domains involving optimization. BL parameterizes a compositional utility function built from intrinsically interpretable modular blocks, which induces a data distribution for prediction and generation. Each block represents and can be written in symbolic form as a utility maximization problem (UMP), a foundational paradigm in behavioral science and a universal framework of optimization. BL supports architectures ranging from a single UMP to hierarchical compositions, the latter modeling hierarchical optimization structures. Its smooth and monotone variant (IBL) guarantees identifiability. Theoretically, we establish the universal approximation property of BL, and analyze the M-estimation properties of IBL. Empirically, BL demonstrates strong predictive performance, intrinsic interpretability and scalability to high-dimensional data. Code: MoonYLiang/Behavior-Learning on GitHub; install via pip install blnetwork.

Why do we need Behavior Learning?

Scientific research often grapples with phenomena that resist precise formalization, including human and social domains. Such phenomena are difficult to predict and even harder to falsify through theory alone. Interpretable machine learning offers a promising alternative, yet a long-standing tension remains: predictive performance and intrinsic interpretability often trade off.

Existing efforts to reconcile these goals have made progress. But the BL paper identifies two further limitations that restrict their scientific applicability.

Limitation 01

Insufficient alignment with scientific theories

Interpretability is often added to existing ML methods rather than grounded in scientific structures, such as optimization problems or differential equations.

Limitation 02

Non-uniqueness of interpretations

Non-identifiable models may admit distinct internal explanations for the same observable predictions, complicating ground-truth parameter estimation.

Can we design an interpretable ML framework that mitigates the performance–interpretability trade-off while being scientifically grounded and identifiable?

BL answers this question by returning to a foundational paradigm of behavioral science: utility maximization.

From behavior to optimization

In behavioral science and decision theory, observed outcomes are often modeled as arising from a latent optimization process. Given contextual features \(\mathbf{x}\), an agent chooses a response \(\mathbf{y}\) by solving a utility maximization problem (UMP):

The utility maximization problem
\[\max_{\mathbf y\in\mathcal Y}\; U(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathcal C(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\leq 0,\quad \mathcal T(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=0.\]
Utility · \(U\)

The agent's internal preferences or goals.

Inequality · \(\mathcal C\)

Constraints such as limited resources or budgets.

Equality · \(\mathcal T\)

Endogenous belief consistency or exogenous conservation laws.

Although BL is inspired by human behavior, the UMP is not domain-specific. The paper shows that constrained maximization and minimization problems can be equivalently expressed as UMPs, opening the door to applications in economics, statistical physics, evolutionary biology, and other domains involving optimization.

Learning a compositional utility function

BL models responses as sampled from a distribution induced by one UMP or a composition of interacting UMPs. Instead of fitting only a direct mapping from inputs to predictions, it learns the compositional utility \(\mathrm{BL}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\), which parameterizes a conditional Gibbs distribution.

Prediction and generation
\[p_{\tau}(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x;\Theta)=\frac{\exp\!\left(\mathrm{BL}_{\Theta}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)/\tau\right)}{Z_{\tau}(\mathbf x;\Theta)}.\]

The temperature \(\tau>0\) controls response randomness. As \(\tau\to0\), the distribution concentrates on utility-maximizing responses. This makes the same learned structure useful for both prediction and generation.

Each learnable block is an optimization problem

The compositional utility is constructed from interpretable modular blocks \(\mathcal B\). Each block represents a penalty-based UMP: an objective, reduced by penalties for violating its constraints.

An interpretable BL block
\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal B(\mathbf x,\mathbf y;\theta)={}&\;\lambda_0^\top\phi\!\left(U_{\theta_U}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\right)\\[-1pt]&-\lambda_1^\top\rho\!\left(\mathcal C_{\theta_C}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\right)\\[-1pt]&-\lambda_2^\top\psi\!\left(\mathcal T_{\theta_T}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\right).\end{aligned}\]

The implementation uses polynomial feature maps, with tanh for utility, ReLU for inequality penalties, and absolute value for equality penalties. Polynomial terms make objectives and constraints traceable and expressible symbolically.

One problem, or an entire hierarchy

BL composes these blocks into increasingly expressive optimization structures while preserving a clear interpretation of the underlying modules.

BL(Single)

One learnable block. A single UMP with maximal structural transparency.

BL(Shallow)

A modest composition of interacting UMPs, with one or two intermediate layers.

BL(Deep)

Multiple layers of UMP blocks. Their composition forms a hierarchical optimization structure.

Interpretation in BL(Deep): bottom-layer modules encode local objectives; higher layers aggregate and coordinate them into coarse-grained optimization structures.

Expressivity, interpretability, and identifiability

BL is designed to unify three objectives: predictive performance, intrinsic interpretability, and statistically meaningful identification. Its theoretical results clarify what each guarantee requires.

Universal approximation of BL

For compact input and output domains and a strictly positive continuous conditional density, a sufficiently expressive finite BL architecture can approximate the target conditional distribution arbitrarily closely in uniform conditional KL divergence.

\[\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal X}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p^\star(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_\tau(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x};\theta^\star)\right)<\varepsilon.\]

Theorem: Universal Approximation of BL. Required depth and width may depend on the approximation tolerance.

Identifiable Behavior Learning (IBL)

Interpretability alone is not identification. To address non-unique parameter explanations, the paper introduces IBL, a smooth, monotone variant of BL. IBL replaces the standard penalty nonlinearities with differentiable alternatives, including softplus and a squared equality penalty.

A practical IBL block
\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal B^{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)={}&\lambda_0^\top\tanh\!\left(\mathbf p_u(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\right)\\&-\lambda_1^\top\operatorname{softplus}\!\left(\mathbf p_c(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\right)\\&-\lambda_2^\top\mathbf p_t(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)^{\odot2}.\end{aligned}\]

Identifiability of IBL

Under the paper's atomic injectivity, linear-independence, and minimal-representation assumption, IBL(Single), IBL(Shallow), and IBL(Deep) are identifiable in the parameter quotient space: parameters are determined up to the specified equivalence class.

Under additional conditions, the paper establishes consistency, universal consistency of the learned conditional distribution, asymptotic normality, and asymptotic efficiency.

These guarantees are conditional theoretical statements, not claims that an arbitrary deep BL parameterization is uniquely recovered from every finite dataset. See the paper for exact assumptions and proofs.

What does BL learn in practice?

The paper examines prediction, symbolic and hierarchical interpretation, scalability to high-dimensional inputs, and penalty-based constraint enforcement.

1. Predictive performance on tabular data

Across 10 datasets and 10 baseline models, BL reaches first-tier predictive performance. In the paper's comparison, it achieves the strongest results among the intrinsically interpretable methods, and BL(Shallow) surpasses the MLP baseline.

2. From a learned model to a symbolic UMP

On the Boston Housing dataset, BL(Single) is interpreted as a representative buyer's optimization problem, with explicit preference, budget, and belief terms. A shallow BL[2,1] recovers economic-sensitive and location-sensitive preference types; BL[5,3,1] reveals a deeper micro-to-macro hierarchy.

For instance, the paper reports a simplified utility polynomial containing the interaction between housing price and room count:

Example: recovered symbolic preference terms
\[\mathbf p_u\approx-0.56P^2-0.6\mathrm{RM}+0.57\mathrm{RM}\cdot P+\widetilde{R}_u.\]

The omitted terms are absorbed into \(\widetilde{R}_u\); this is an approximate extracted expression, not a claim of exact structural identification.

3. Scaling to image and text inputs

With parameter-matched energy-based MLP (E-MLP) comparisons, BL shows comparable in-distribution accuracy on image tasks and improved in-distribution accuracy across the tested text tasks. Out-of-distribution detection is dataset-dependent: BL does better on Yelp, while E-MLP performs better on AG News. BL also reports favorable calibration; training time is generally somewhat higher.

4. Does the learned penalty enforce constraints?

In a 64-dimensional energy-conservation diagnostic, the paper isolates BL's equality-penalty mechanism. At the tested finite temperatures, conservation violations generally decline with decreasing temperature or increasing penalty weight.

Reading a learned hierarchy as science

The paper describes a bottom-up interpretation of BL(Deep). Each block is a local optimization problem; higher layers summarize, reorganize, and coordinate the structures learned by lower layers. Analogies include organizational hierarchies, representative agents, and coarse-graining in statistical physics.

Raw input features→Micro-level UMP blocks→Coarse-grained constructs→Macro-level systems

In this view, BL is not merely a predictor with post-hoc explanations: its interpretable optimization blocks are the model itself. The resulting hierarchies offer candidate scientific descriptions to inspect, compare, and test.
